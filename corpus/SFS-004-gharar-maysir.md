# SFS-004 — Gharar and Maysir: Uncertainty, Speculation, and Insurance

## §1 Scope

§1.1 This document addresses *gharar* (excessive uncertainty in a contract) and
*maysir* (gambling), and applies them to derivatives, insurance, and speculative
products.

## §2 Gharar

§2.1 *Gharar* is uncertainty or ambiguity in the essential terms of a contract —
the subject matter, the price, the quantity, the time of delivery, or whether
delivery will occur at all.

§2.2 Only *excessive* gharar invalidates a contract. Minor uncertainty that is
unavoidable in ordinary commerce is tolerated.

§2.3 Gharar is excessive where it concerns a fundamental term, where it is avoidable
by better specification, and where it exposes a party to loss they could not
reasonably assess at contracting.

§2.4 Selling what one does not possess and cannot reliably deliver is a paradigm
case of excessive gharar. Compare the ownership requirement at [SFS-002 §2.2].

## §3 Maysir

§3.1 *Maysir* is the acquisition of wealth by chance at another's expense, where
each party stands to win only if the other loses.

§3.2 A transaction is maysir where the outcome depends on an uncertain future event,
the parties' positions are strictly opposed, and no productive activity or genuine
risk transfer underlies it.

§3.3 Conventional options, futures held purely for speculation, contracts for
difference, and spread betting fall within maysir and are not permissible.

§3.4 Hedging a genuine, existing commercial exposure is distinguishable from
speculation. Instruments used to hedge an actual underlying position should be
referred for review rather than rejected automatically, since permissibility turns
on the presence of the exposure and the structure of the instrument.

## §4 Insurance

§4.1 Conventional commercial insurance is widely held to be impermissible because it
combines gharar (the premium buys an uncertain and possibly non-existent payout),
maysir (the insurer gains if no claim arises, the insured gains if one does), and
riba (premium reserves invested in interest-bearing instruments).

§4.2 *Takaful* is the permissible alternative: participants contribute to a mutual
fund on the basis of donation (*tabarru'*), losses are met from the pooled fund, the
operator manages it for a fee or profit share rather than underwriting the risk, and
surplus is returned to participants.

§4.3 A takaful arrangement whose operator guarantees a payout from its own capital,
or retains the underwriting surplus as profit, reproduces the defects at §4.1.

## §5 Assessment criteria

§5.1 A product should be judged non-compliant where its return depends wholly on an
uncertain event with no underlying asset or productive activity.

§5.2 A product should be judged non-compliant where it is structured as a zero-sum
wager between the institution and the customer.

§5.3 A product should be referred for review where uncertainty exists but may be
reducible by clearer specification of subject matter, price, or delivery.

§5.4 Ordinary commercial uncertainty — the possibility that a genuine investment
performs poorly — is not gharar. A Mudarabah account whose return varies with
performance is permissible on this ground. See [SFS-005 §2].
